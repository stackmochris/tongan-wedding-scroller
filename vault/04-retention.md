# Second Language Development and Retention

## The Forgetting Curve

Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885) established that memory decays rapidly without reinforcement. Approximate savings (relearning effort reduced) from classic data:

- ~58% retained after 20 minutes
- ~44% after 1 hour
- ~34% after 1 day
- ~21% after 31 days

Meaningful material decays more slowly than nonsense syllables. Spaced review flattens the curve.

## Spacing Effect

Meta-analytic evidence is strong:

- Cepeda et al. (2006): 839 comparisons across 317 experiments; spaced practice reliably outperforms massed practice.
- Kim & Webb (2022): meta-analysis of 48 experiments (N=3,411) on L2 learning; medium-to-large effect of spacing. Longer spacing better for delayed retention.

Expanding and equal intervals are roughly equivalent in many studies; the key is distribution over time rather than massing.

## Combining Techniques

Studies combining retrieval practice, spacing, and feedback show large gains relative to typical study-then-cram patterns (e.g., ~18 percentage-point accuracy increase vs ~8 in one classroom-laboratory design with Turkish learners of English).

Long-term retention (years) is supported by initial spacing of weeks rather than days in classic Bahrick work on Spanish vocabulary.

## Implications for Design

- First review within 24 hours of initial exposure.
- Subsequent reviews at expanding intervals (days → weeks → months).
- Prefer retrieval (quiz, recall) over passive re-reading.
- Embed new items in meaningful sentences or stories to slow decay.
- Track individual item difficulty to prioritise review.
- Portable across languages: memory mechanisms are general; adapt scheduling to item complexity and learner level.

**Sources**: Ebbinghaus (1885); Cepeda et al. (2006); Kim & Webb (2022); Bahrick studies on long-term retention; multidomain retrieval + spacing experiments.
